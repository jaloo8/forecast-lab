## 1. What thirteen percent a year hides

**Narration:** Thirteen percent a year sounds impressive. But what happened in the losing years? We calculated twenty years of published US stock-portfolio returns. From 2006 through 2025, larger companies with high operating profitability compounded at about thirteen-point-three percent a year, gross. The broad-market research benchmark earned about eleven-point-one percent. But that profitability portfolio lost nearly twenty-nine percent in 2008, and it had three losing years altogether. A hypothetical two-percent annual cost reduced its compound return to about eleven percent. These are published research portfolios, not our live trading results. The next test is whether individual-stock histories, realistic trades and costs support the finding. That includes companies that failed. Follow the research, including the misses.

## 2. Costco: strong business, attractive price?

**Narration:** A strong business and an attractively priced stock are different questions. Costco reported eleven-percent growth in fourth-quarter revenue. Its annual operating cash flow, less cash spending on property and equipment, was about nine-point-four billion dollars. That is useful evidence of demand and cash generation. But using our October ninth reference share price, the stock traded at about forty-six times the year's reported earnings. Annual cash flow divided by market value was only about two-point-two percent. That is a valuation measure, not your expected return. The question is how much future growth the price already assumes. Our watchlist tracks sales, membership economics and cash conversion. Strong operations earn further research; they do not make any entry price attractive.

## 3. Target: separate operating recovery from a refund

**Narration:** A big jump in earnings can have more than one explanation. Target reported second-quarter sales growth of five-point-three percent and a nine-point-six percent operating margin. Traffic increased, which supports a recovery case. But the quarter also included almost one billion dollars of tariff refunds. Removing that disclosed benefit reduces the operating margin to roughly five-point-nine percent. That does not erase the improvement in traffic. It changes how we judge repeatable profit. Our research question is whether customers keep returning and margins improve without another refund. We would reconsider the recovery thesis if traffic turns negative in two consecutive quarters or the margin excluding refunds falls below five percent. Those are our test conditions, not a prediction of the next stock move.
