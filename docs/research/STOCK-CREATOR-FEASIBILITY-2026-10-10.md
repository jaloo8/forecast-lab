# Stock-research creator feasibility — 2026-10-10

Scope: a personal Instagram research publication using original analysis, edited Reels, and AI narration, with possible later monetization and YouTube Shorts distribution. US rules are the working assumption; the owner's residence, audience jurisdictions, account status, and specific commercial arrangements are unconfirmed. Research only: no accounts, purchases, messages, or publication were performed.

## Assessment

**Feasible as a small editorial experiment; revenue and account eligibility are unproven.** Recommended positioning is a human-owned research publication with traceable sources and clearly identified synthetic narration. Avoid a fabricated expert persona. Test whether viewers value the research before budgeting on platform payouts. This is a business inference from the policies below, not a prediction of growth or earnings.

## Originality and AI

- **Instagram originality:** Meta's [2024 Instagram recommendation announcement](https://about.fb.com/ltam/news/2024/05/ayudando-a-los-creadores-a-encontrar-nuevas-audiencias/) describes preferring the original over identical reposts and excluding aggregators from recommendation surfaces. It distinguishes meaningful transformation from identical copies. This is historical recommendation guidance, not a blanket monetization guarantee. The [current original-content guidelines](https://creators.instagram.com/original-content-guidelines) were inaccessible in this research session. Do not substitute Facebook-specific monetization rules for Instagram rules.
- **Meta AI disclosure:** Meta's [AI-labeling announcement](https://about.fb.com/news/2024/02/labeling-ai-generated-images-on-facebook-instagram-and-threads/) says users must use its disclosure tool for organic photorealistic video or realistic-sounding audio digitally created or altered. Its [subsequent labeling update](https://about.fb.com/news/2024/04/metas-approach-to-labeling-ai-generated-content-and-manipulated-media/) describes the AI-info approach. These are published platform statements, rather than an inspection of the owner's current upload UI. Practical choice: label realistic synthetic narration and describe it plainly in the content.
- **YouTube monetization, live policy checked:** originality and substantive variation matter; generic mass-produced AI templates, minimal alterations to others' clips, and readings of others' website/news text can fail monetization review. Permission to reuse footage does not itself satisfy the separate reused-content policy. Crucially, the policy explicitly excludes AI personas presented as human experts on sensitive subjects, including AI podcast hosts offering investment guidance. A synthetic voice reading human-authored research is not explicitly equated with every prohibited persona, but approval is not guaranteed. [YouTube channel monetization policies](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392?hl=en).
- **YouTube disclosure, live policy checked:** realistic, meaningfully AI-generated or altered content requires disclosure; creating scripts/captions and cloning one's own voice for narration are listed as examples not requiring it. YouTube says disclosure itself does not reduce monetization eligibility or audience reach. The separate content policies still apply. [YouTube GenAI disclosure guidance](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491?hl=en).

Editorial inference: use your own argument, calculations, charts, and verified source extracts; license any third-party assets; use narration as a production aid. A labeled AI narrator is a better experiment than a fictional analyst with invented qualifications. Neither AI labels nor editing establish copyright permission.

## US commercial boundaries

**Sponsorship disclosure.** FTC guidance covers material relationships including money, free products, employment, family, and other valuable benefits. Disclosures should accompany the endorsement, be difficult to miss, and appear inside a video rather than only in its caption. Both audible and visual disclosure are useful. A platform's partnership label may need supplementary disclosure. [FTC Disclosures 101](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/disclosures-101-social-media-influencers). Meta also states that paid partnerships involving value exchange must be labeled; see its [published branded-content explanation](https://about.fb.com/fr/news/2023/02/comment-meta-accompagne-les-createurs-de-contenus-et-les-acteurs-de-linfluence-en-ligne/).

**Paid stock promotion is a separate issue.** The SEC says securities endorsements may be unlawful without disclosure of the nature, source, and amount of compensation from the company. A generic advertisement label is not a substitute for securities-specific disclosure. Its [2017 statement](https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/speeches-statements/statement-potentially-unlawful-promotion-icos-sec-statement-urging-caution-around-celebrity-backed-icos) discusses stocks as well as token offerings; its [stock-article enforcement announcement](https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2017-79) documents action against paid publicity disguised as independent research. These are older SEC statements/enforcement evidence, not newly enacted rules.

**Paid securities research is not automatically exempt from adviser rules.** The SEC's [2015 publisher-exclusion interpretation](https://www.sec.gov/divisions/investment/noaction/2015/jonathon-hendricks-012615-202a.htm) describes three factual requirements: impersonal advice, genuine/disinterested analysis, and general/regular circulation rather than publication timed to specific market events. The staff declined to decide that applicant's status without sufficient facts. A public page, subscription, or “not financial advice” statement does not itself resolve the analysis. Personalized portfolio replies, paid buy/sell alerts, promotional relationships, and publication timing deserve review before commercialization. Federal and state requirements depend on the actual business and jurisdiction; this note makes no registration conclusion.

**Promoting an adviser adds another layer.** The SEC's [investment-adviser marketing guide](https://www.sec.gov/resources-small-businesses/small-business-compliance-guides/investment-adviser-marketing) describes disclosure, oversight, and other conditions for compensated endorsements used by SEC-registered or required-to-register advisers. Those rules should not be generalized to every ordinary creator, but adviser referral sponsorships require specific review.

## Suggested pilot and decision points

The following are recommendations, not platform requirements:

1. Produce a small batch of original, human-reviewed research Reels. Each should show the question, dated evidence, uncertainty, and what would invalidate the thesis.
2. Maintain a source record and distinguish historical performance, hypothetical examples, and actual investments. Disclose material holdings/conflicts where relevant; never invent expert credentials or results.
3. Measure completion, saves, meaningful comments, and repeat viewers alongside production time. Do not set a revenue target from unsupported RPM estimates.
4. Consider general education or research-process content before individualized advice, transaction alerts, or issuer-sponsored recommendations. If monetizing securities analysis, review the actual product and jurisdiction before launch.

Unresolved: account/country eligibility; current Meta commercial terms; audience demand and willingness to pay; rights to chart/data redistribution, music, footage, and synthetic voices; applicable state law; employer outside-activity/confidentiality requirements. These are concrete commercialization questions, not reasons to delay a private editorial prototype.

## A concrete 30-day experiment

Positioning hypothesis: **company research in plain English, with evidence and follow-up**. The page can showcase reasoning while proprietary source code remains private. Public explanations necessarily reveal some ideas; no publishing format prevents copying. Publish only data/visuals whose licenses permit the intended use. In particular, a personal-only market-data subscription should not be assumed suitable for a monetized publication.

- Week 1: privately produce three 45–75-second drafts; compare two authorized narrator samples using the same paragraph. Check ticker/number pronunciation, pacing, mobile readability, accurate captions and music rights. Estimated setup/research/editing time is a planning budget of 4–6 hours/week, not measured throughput.
- Weeks 1–4, after owner approval to publish: three distinct Reels weekly, with a clean master for YouTube Shorts. A reusable visual style is fine; each episode needs substantive original analysis. No publication or automation is authorized by this note.
- Mix: four company-quality/moat explainers, four catalyst/expectation explainers, and four research tests or follow-ups including failures. Individual-company episodes need fresh primary-source research before scripting.
- For each episode retain the dated source links, factual claims, calculation inputs, narrator/asset license, disclosure needs and human review. Show bull case, bear case and what would change the conclusion where a company is discussed.
- Measure completion relative to video length, saves/shares per reached account, profile visits, repeat viewers, voluntary email signups and production time. Compare formats after 12 posts; follower count alone is not the success test. No universal retention/conversion threshold is invented.
- Continue only if production is sustainable and there are repeated signs of demand. Revise weak formats based on evidence before buying a larger software stack or paid audience acquisition. This is a proposal, not a scheduled automation.

Possible first topics: “What 13% annualized hides about losing years”; “A good company can still be an expensive stock”; “When earnings beat expectations but guidance disappoints.” The first uses completed research; the other two require their own factual examples before filming. Neither profit screenshots nor selected winning calls should substitute for a complete track record.

## Revenue model to test

Start with free original analysis and a free written summary; later test demand for deeper, regularly published research, educational material or tools, subject to product-specific legal review. Optional sponsorships/affiliates should be for products actually evaluated, with clear disclosures. Recommendation: do not build the business around issuer-paid stock promotions or assumed Instagram view payouts. An external email audience reduces dependence on one platform, but it requires permission-based signup and its own operating work.

Illustrative subscription arithmetic only, not expected earnings: 50 paying readers at $10/month = $500/month gross; 200 = $2,000/month gross. Neither example assumes a follower conversion rate. Deduct platform/payment fees, refunds, taxes, software, data, legal costs and the owner's time. Followers, video views, subscribers and retained paying customers are different measures. Budget first-month revenue at zero for planning; the content experiment can still supply evidence of audience demand.

Before charging for securities analysis, evaluate the actual offering, jurisdictions and employer outside-activity requirements. Calling a paid product “education” or adding “not financial advice” does not itself decide whether it falls under adviser rules. The current research and private prototypes can proceed without assuming commercialization is already cleared.

## First private draft: 60-second research Reel

**Working title:** What 13% annualized hides. **Format:** faceless original charts with disclosed synthetic narration. **Status:** script/storyboard only; no audio/video rendered or published.

Narration:

> Thirteen percent a year sounds impressive. But what happens in the losing years? We compared twenty years of published US stock portfolios. From 2006 through 2025, larger companies with high operating profitability compounded at about thirteen-point-three percent a year, before our trading costs and taxes. The broad-market research benchmark earned about eleven-point-one percent. But that profitability portfolio still lost nearly twenty-nine percent in 2008. And a hypothetical two-percent yearly cost nearly erased its advantage. This is a useful research lead. It is not our live trading record, and it is not a promise about next year. Our next question is whether company-by-company testing with realistic trades supports the result. Follow the research, including the misses.

Storyboard: 0–6s show the question, without a promise; 6–24s compare 13.27% and 11.06% gross CAGR with 2006–2025 clearly visible; 24–38s show the -28.55% 2008 loss; 38–49s show 11.01% CAGR under the hypothetical 2% annual wealth drag; 49–60s state “published research portfolios, not live results” and show the next research question. The cost drag is an assumption, not measured turnover cost. Spoken duration requires checking with the chosen voice; adjust timing after rendering.

Evidence: [our complete calculation and limitations](FREE-STOCK-FACTOR-SCREEN-2026-10-10.md), [Kenneth French's library](https://mba.tuck.dartmouth.edu/pages/faculty/ken.french/data_library.html). Before publication, verify the final graphic numbers against the saved CSVs, retain clear gross/simulation labels, confirm source/data display rights and apply platform disclosure requirements. No stock-specific recommendation is made in this draft.
